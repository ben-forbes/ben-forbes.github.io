Human-Centered Bimanual Teleoperation

Human Fusions Institute (UCLA, Case Western, Cleveland State) · IEEE Haptics Symposium poster, 2026

A two-arm robot that a person operates through a passthrough headset across large distances, seeing through the robot’s camera and still feeling what it touches, and a shared autonomy controller that learns from each operator.

My part: I lead the controls team. I wrote the teleoperation controller and run the shared autonomy study.

Video · Poster (PDF)

The system

The Office of Naval Research funds this collaboration between UCLA, Case Western Reserve University and Cleveland State University. The system pairs a 7-DOF Franka Emika FR3 with a 6-DOF Universal Robots UR5e. Each arm carries a gripper instrumented with vision-based tactile sensors, and an egocentric camera streams the scene to the operator’s headset.

How the person and the robot connect

Four channels tie the operator to the robot. Moving the wrist moves the arm, and closing the hand closes the gripper.

When the gripper’s tactile sensors touch something, the operator feels it on their hand through neurohaptic display rings designed by our collaborators at Case Western.

Turning the head turns the camera, and its view streams back to the headset.

Teleoperation controller

The controller had to:

  • Avoid kinematic singularities
  • Maximize manipulability of the end effector
  • Support both “palm up” and “palm down” hand configurations
  • Keep up with human movements

I developed an optimization-based controller that maximizes manipulability and minimizes joint velocities within each arm’s kinematic limits, keeping the robot responsive and predictable so the operator feels embodied in it. It runs in ROS 2 across both arms.

Learning-based shared autonomy

Shared autonomy systems are usually judged on task speed and accuracy. My research asks how they feel to the person in the loop: sense of agency, trust and embodiment. Touch anchors the operator’s presence while working remotely.

While participants teleoperate, we capture eye gaze (direction, pupil diameter, focus distance) from a Varjo headset, ECG, and head, wrist and finger kinematics, as windows into the operator’s experience, not just markers of performance.

In Phase 1, participants perform the task with direct teleoperation. Their data trains a personalized shared autonomy controller that incorporates gaze and tactile information. In Phase 2, they return and repeat the task under randomized A/B conditions, shared autonomy versus direct teleoperation, with workload and trust surveys alongside the multimodal data.

The task is deliberately open-ended: place pulleys, thread a rope, and clip a carabiner to move a breakable object to a goal, a problem that rewards human creativity and long-horizon planning.

Full demo

One continuous take at 1× speed: an operator wearing the passthrough headset completes the five-step pulley task (clip the carabiner, route the rope, close the pulley, hang it on the hook, and pull the rope to trigger the pulley). Panels show the robot, the headset view and eyes, tactile feedback, and heart rate.
